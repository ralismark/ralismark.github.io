> _All the world will be your enemy, Prince with a Thousand Enemies, and whenever they catch you, they will kill you._
>
> _But first they must catch you, digger, listener, runner, prince with the swift warning._
>
> _Be cunning and full of tricks and your people shall never be destroyed._

Such an epic tale, woven together with such artful use of the English language.
There's genuinely so much about this novel that I love, both the narrative itself as well as how it's written are incredible and inspiring.
No word is misplaced, everything aligns to immerse us in the rabbits' ventures in the world and paint it in such epic light.
There's so much that I wanna point out that I struggled writing this review!

I loved the Lapine (rabbit-kind) folklore so much!
I have [such a soft spot](/soupworld) for these kinds of myths, and the ones in the novel have such a powerful message: the rabbits may be a people with innumerable enemies, yes, but innumerable enemies that can be outwitted and escaped.
It's that absolute banger of a quote that I've put at the top, which I see every so often on Tumblr in the context of queer survival.
The creation myth really drives home how the rabbits see themselves as a people that must find a life in the space between Frith (the sun/god) that created the world, the elil[^elil] he created to curb their numbers, and the Black Rabbit of Inle that guides those whose time it is to "stop running".
A people led by the mythical El-ahrairah, who embodies their values of cunning and leadership and hope, and maybe even a little bit of arrogance.
But especially hope, with how these stories are told to raise spirits and inspire determination in the rabbits, and by proxy, us.
It makes the rabbits so much more than just mere protagonists, but a society with _history_ and an _oral tradition_.

[^elil]: _elil_ is the Lapine word for enemy: dogs, cat, foxes, weasels, birds, and other animals that prey on the rabbits.
	And the most dangerous of them all: humans.

As a recovering languages nerd I tend to be too critical of fictional languages in media.
This book is no exception (don't expect a full conlang out of it) but of course this isn't the point of the language, which is to further show how the rabbits view the world, with elil which threaten them, _hrududil_ which overpower them, and _-rah_ which lead them.
And it does so really well, being so much more than just a decorative element: "My heart has joined the Thousand, for my friend stopped running today" OH MY GOD.
It really lends more body to the rabbits' world, and makes this novel feel like a mini-Tolkein.

I really want to talk about how well the folklore is blended into the narrative, since I think this one of the main reasons this novel feels so _epic_.
There's so many parallels between the folk tales and the wider narrative!
The whole novel leading up to the Efrafan heist portrays El-ahrairah as so much larger than life (as characters in myths are), and in a very "hero's journey"-esque way the Watership rabbits hatch a plan inspired by them, culminating in them appearing just as mythical as they execute their plot -- which they need to be to challenge the Orwellian Efrafa warren and the great General Woundwort that holds it under his paw.
The Efrafans are witness to how, in completely opposition to being a threat to the Watership rabbits, elil submit to them: a _homba_ (fox), a black-headed gull, a mouse, and eventually even a _dog_.
Even _lightning_ is portrayed as being on the side of Bigwig!
Reality grows to the level of myth as the Efrafan chapters unfold.

Even despite the density of references to landforms and flora and fauna that are specific to England (and are thus inscrutable to me), the descriptions of nature in this book are so vivid, and so effective in setting the atmosphere of scenes and showing us the world from the perspective of the rabbits.
The moonlight pouring over the down making everything glimmer.
The little creek that becomes almost an inescapable rapid.
A few miles' journey that would be a gentle stroll for a human becomes long and treacherous.
Everything is so much grander, everything so much more treacherous, such is the world when you're a little rabbit (and [they're being so brave about it!](https://www.tumblr.com/shinelikedew/779855695190360064/i-love-so-much-how-adams-really-conveys-the-stress)).
And nature -- and especially the weather and the day/night cycle -- are so dynamic, and just as much of a character that accompanies the rabbits on their quest.
There's so much rain-based pathetic fallacy, which one of my favourite literary techniques!
We get as much of a intuitive sense of the atmosphere and vibe of locations from the descriptions, just as the rabbits would from their environment.

---

Cowslip's Warren is such a great showcase of this vibe-setting, and uses it to great effect in its messaging about the tradeoff between safety and freedom.
Rabbits are born to be free in nature, and this is what they know in their hearts to be right, but the warrens we encounter forsake this nature.
Efrafa is such an obvious analogy for police states, and you can draw so many parallels with _Nineteen Eighty Four_, so I won't go into it much.
Cowslip's Warren, however, is very interesting: they are under the protection of humans, who provide quality food and kill all predators.
This life of comfort would be ideal, but right from the start every aspect of the world is uneasy, every possible sign points to something being wrong.
Fiver is obviously freaking out, Hazel is having bad dreams, and also _the world outside is off_.
Not just the rain, but:

> It had turned chilly.
> Across the field the bloom of the cherry tree under which they had sat that morning hung sodden and spoiled.
> [...]
> The fields and woods were shut in under [the rain], emptied and subdued.
> The insect life of the leaves and grass was stilled.
> **The thrush should have been singing, but Hazel could hear no thrush.**

All this suspenseful build-up until the reveal of the snare, the one wrinkle in their otherwise idyllic life.
It is this constantly looming, unpredictable, and unmitigable threat that they cannot ignore no matter how much they desire so, that drives them to madness and to forsake Frith.
And with this awareness, the Watership rabbits depart, once again demonstrating the value of true freedom, even with its natural dangers of the world[^trauma].

[^trauma]: This arc speaks to me a lot, as someone that essentially had to think about the same tradeoff.
	No details because it's quite personal, but you'll be glad to know I chose the true freedom.
	Also reminds me of _myhouse.wad_'s fake comfort (fake beach) you must forsake and the real happiness (true ending) you must fight for.

---

This book review has gone on a tad too long, but of course that would happen for my personal _Lord of the Rings_.

There was one thing I didn't like, which was the mangled English for the other animals and the humans.
This way of writing dialects ends up being way too obtrusive and hard to understand, and often I wasn't sure if I was understanding them right.
The British terms for locations and flora/fauna species were also something I couldn't understand, but it's not something I can really fault the novel for.

I didn't mention the narrative much because it's been a week between finishing the book and writing this review and my mind is a leaky sponge that has already simplified my recollection of it.
But the plot is really lovely and paced really well, I have no complaints.

Definitely a book I would love a physical copy of so I can revisit it.
And ideally eventually be inspiration for my English writing -- I remember, while reading one of the earlier chapters, having a moment of insight in appreciating the sentence construction.
Probably also thematic inspiration for future Soupworld (or even my personal life) bits.

And of course, I desperately need _Tales from Watership Down_ now...
